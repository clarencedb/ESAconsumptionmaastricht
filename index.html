About

Consumption is a domain in which inequalities become visible, material and lived. What people are able to eat, wear, inhabit, access, repair or share depends on the distribution of economic resources, time, knowledge, infrastructures and social recognition. Consumption can signal and reproduce privilege and exclusion, but it can also become a terrain of solidarity, collective experimentation and demands for greater equality.

The 2027 Midterm Conference of the European Sociological Association's Research Network 05, Sociology of Consumption, invites participants to explore how inequalities are produced, experienced, justified and contested through consumption. It opens with an Early Career Workshop on Monday 18 October, followed by three days of papers, roundtables and working sessions from 19 to 21 October 2027, hosted by the Maastricht Sustainability Institute at Maastricht University's School of Business and Economics.

Under what conditions does consumption reproduce privilege, vulnerability and exclusion, and how might it contribute to more democratic, sustainable and equitable forms of social organisation?

Addressing these questions requires attention not only to differences in what people consume, but also to the conditions that shape and justify them. We propose three entry points.

  1. Moral classifications

    How consumption is judged

    The judgements through which consumption is classed as necessary or excessive, responsible or irresponsible. Who defines “good” consumption, whose practices gain legitimacy, and how is responsibility shared among individuals, households, businesses and public institutions?

  2. Provisioning systems

    How access is organised

    The institutions, infrastructures, policies, material stocks and labour that make food, housing, energy, mobility and care available, from markets and the state to mutual aid, foraging, gifting and other informal practices that are unevenly accessible and differently valued.

  3. Just transitions

    How sustainable futures are pursued or denied

    Ecological transitions are not automatically fair. A consumption lens asks who benefits, who bears the costs and who shapes change, and what welfare institutions, public infrastructures and collective provisioning can do to make transitions just.

Call for papers

We invite proposals for individual papers, paper sessions, lightning talks, roundtables and working sessions. Contributions may draw on any approach: qualitative, quantitative, computational, historical, participatory, mixed-methods or theoretical.

While inequality provides the theme, we warmly welcome contributions from across the full range of the sociology of consumption, and particularly encourage work from regions and traditions underrepresented in European consumption research, including Central and Eastern Europe and the Global South.

Possible themes

  • Inequalities, privilege and exclusion
  • Moralities and the politics of “good” consumption
  • Infrastructures, welfare and provisioning: food, housing, energy, mobility, care
  • Collective, informal and non-market provisioning
  • Sustainable consumption and just transitions
  • Interspecies justice
  • Political consumerism and social movements
  • Political economy of consumption
  • Labour, livelihoods and global supply chains
  • Consumer empowerment and vulnerability
  • Class, capitals and cultural stratification
  • Distinction, identity and taste
  • Gender, race, disability and intersecting inequalities
  • Generations and the life course
  • Changing lifestyles
  • Geographies of consumer culture
  • Central and Eastern Europe and the Global South
  • Digital consumption, platformisation and datafication
  • Materiality and immateriality
  • Bodies, emotions and the senses
  • Compulsive and excessive consumption
  • Leisure, tourism and cultural consumption
  • Neglected and emerging topics, theories and methods

What to submit

  • In English, via Aanmelder
  • A title, a main text of no more than 250 words, and three to five keywords
  • Names, affiliations and email addresses of all authors
  • One paper per presenting author
  • Session, roundtable and working-session proposals: a session abstract of up to 250 words on aims and format, with organisers and confirmed participants

How we evaluate

  • Sociological background of the research
  • Research questions and methodology
  • Key findings, or expected results
  • Implications and contributions
